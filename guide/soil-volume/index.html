What it is#

Soil volume is the amount of uncompacted soil a tree’s roots can reach. In a parking lot it is the problem: a tree in a small paved island is, in the words of a Virginia Tech fact sheet, in a kind of pseudo-container.[1]

A published guideline#

The Virginia Tech fact sheet advises designing lot islands with as much soil volume as possible, ideally letting roots grow at least to the drip line, the edge of the crown, at maturity. It recommends 2 to 3 cubic feet of soil per 1 square foot of crown spread.[1]

Worked example (my arithmetic)

Take a tree whose mature crown is 30 feet across. The crown covers about π × 15² ≈ 707 square feet. At 2–3 cubic feet per square foot, that is about 1,400 to 2,100 cubic feet of soil, or a bed 3 feet deep and about 470 to 700 square feet. That is more than many parking islands provide, which is why soil, not the tree, is the hard part.

A field test#

A study of 1,127 parking lot trees at Walt Disney World found 81% in good condition and only 3% dead since planting, and that every tree in at least 1,500 cubic feet of soil was in good condition. Live oaks (Quercus virginiana) planted in groups did better than those planted singly. The authors also say existing planting recommendations were only imperfectly applicable to the site.[2]

What I do not know#

Limits of this entry

The Disney lots are in Florida with different soil, species and irrigation than a Woodland Hills lot, and the 1,500 cubic feet result is for that sample. I do not know the soil under any Valley lot I have studied. A site would need a soil test and an arborist before I put numbers on a tree.
