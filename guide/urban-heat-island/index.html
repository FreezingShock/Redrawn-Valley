What it is#

An urban heat island is a city or neighborhood that is measurably warmer than the less built-up land around it. The U.S. EPA summarizes the research this way: in the United States, daytime temperatures in urban areas run about 1–7°F higher than in outlying areas, and nighttime temperatures about 2–5°F higher.[1] The gap is usually measured as a difference between a city and its surroundings, so it depends on which surroundings you pick.

A heat island is not the same thing as a heat wave. A heat wave is weather. A heat island is a built-in offset that rides on top of the weather, and it is largest on the calm, clear days when the weather is already dangerous.[1]

Five causes#

The EPA lists five, and each one shows up on an ordinary parking lot:[1]

  • Fewer natural landscapes. Trees, plants and water cool the air by shading, by releasing water from leaves, and by evaporation. Hard, dry surfaces such as roofs, sidewalks, roads and parking lots do less of all three.
  • Material properties. Conventional pavement and roofing reflect less solar energy and absorb and emit more heat than vegetation. Heat islands often build through the day and become stronger after sunset as that stored heat leaks out slowly.
  • Urban geometry. Tall buildings and narrow streets trap heat and block wind.
  • Waste heat. Vehicles, air conditioners, buildings and industry all release heat.
  • Weather and geography. Calm, clear weather makes heat islands worse. Wind and cloud cover suppress them. Nearby mountains can block wind or redirect it.

The first two are the ones a small site can change. That is why my studies spend their effort on tree canopy and on what the ground is made of (impervious surface, cool pavement).

Surface and atmospheric#

The term covers two different measurements. A surface heat island is how hot the ground and roofs themselves get, which is what a satellite sees. The EPA notes that on a warm day conventional roofing can reach as much as 66°F warmer than the surrounding air.[1] An atmospheric heat island is the warmer air, which is what a thermometer in the shade reads. The EPA says atmospheric heat islands vary much less in intensity than surface ones.[1]

This matters for design claims. A pavement that is 15°F cooler at the surface has not made the air 15°F cooler, and a person standing on it feels more than air temperature (see cool pavement).

Why it matters#

The EPA ties heat islands to three costs. Electricity demand for air conditioning rises about 1–9% for each 2°F increase in temperature. Higher demand means more fossil-fuel emissions, and more sun and heat means more ground-level ozone. And hot weather contributes to heat-related illness and death, with older adults, young children, low-income populations, people who work outdoors and people with chronic conditions at greatest risk. From 2004 to 2018 the CDC recorded an average of 702 heat deaths per year in the U.S.[1]

In the Valley#

The Valley is a plausible heat island host: it is inland, ringed by mountains, and summer days there are typically hotter than in the Los Angeles Basin.[2] Its all-time county record is a weather fact, not a heat-island measurement, but it shows the baseline a built-up block starts from: 121°F at Pierce College in Woodland Hills on Sept. 6, 2020.[3]

What I do not know#

Limits of this entry

The 1–7°F and 2–5°F figures are a national range from a review of studies, not a measurement of the Valley. I have not found a published heat island magnitude for a specific Valley block, and I have not measured one. If I want to claim one for a study site, I need paired temperature readings at the site and at a shaded reference, taken the same hour.
