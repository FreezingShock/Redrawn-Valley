What it is#

Tree canopy is the layer of leaves, branches and stems that cover the ground when you look straight down. “Percent canopy” is the share of an area under that layer. It is the standard way cities count their urban forest because it can be estimated from aerial imagery instead of counted tree by tree.

An analysis of land cover data from about 2016 found that 18% of Los Angeles County and 25% of the City of Los Angeles was covered by tree canopy.[1] That 25% is the number I cite for the city in my studies.

It is not spread evenly#

The same report found that five census block groups, one in Pacific Palisades, one in Los Feliz, two in Brentwood and one in Shadow Hills, hold 18% of the city’s total tree canopy while less than 1% of the city’s population lives there.[1] The authors point to a cause: more affluent residents can afford the hill areas, which are better suited to trees and less densely developed.[1] The Union of Concerned Scientists repeats the same pattern, nearly 20% of the urban forest where 1% of people live.[2]

That unevenness is the subject of shade equity.

Canopy is not the same as shade#

This is my own reasoning, not a sourced finding: a percent canopy figure averages over an area, but a person stands at one spot. A neighborhood can have healthy canopy in back yards and none over its bus stops. And a young tree counts as a dot from above while casting almost no shade for years. When I plan planting on a site I therefore ask where the shade lands, at what hour, over what people are doing, and how big the tree will be when it is mature.

Getting a tree to maturity in a paved lot depends mostly on what is underground, which is the subject of soil volume.

What I do not know#

Limits of this entry

The 18% and 25% figures come from one 2016-era land cover analysis and will have changed. I do not have a canopy number for the Valley as a whole, for Woodland Hills, or for any block I have studied. I would need the county’s canopy layer clipped to a site to state one.
