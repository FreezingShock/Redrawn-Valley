What it is#

An impervious surface is one that rain cannot pass through. The USGS gives the everyday examples: superhighways, streets, pavement, driveways and house roofs.[1] Land that is natural or planted can take in rain; hard cover sends it somewhere else.

What happens to the water#

In a forested watershed a significant share of rainfall is absorbed into the soil, stored as groundwater and released slowly to streams. When a watershed urbanizes and vegetation is replaced by impervious cover, there is less area where rain can infiltrate, so more runoff has to be collected by curbs, storm sewers and ditches and carried straight to streams.

In a developed watershed much more water reaches a stream much more quickly, and the USGS states that severe flood events happen more often as impervious area grows.[1]

The heat link#

Impervious cover is also hard and dry, which is why it appears in the EPA’s list of heat island causes: hard, dry surfaces provide less shade and moisture than natural landscapes.[2] A parking lot is both problems at once, which is why I treat permeable paving and bioswales as part of the cooling design and not only the drainage design.

In the Valley#

Two Valley projects show the other direction. On Elmer Avenue in Sun Valley, a street with no pedestrian or stormwater infrastructure and frequent flooding was retrofitted so that bioswales and a buried infiltration gallery capture runoff from a 40-acre area.[3] The Tujunga Wash, a 13-mile tributary of the Los Angeles River, was channelized for flood control in the 1950s;[4] see riparian corridor for the greenway built beside it.
