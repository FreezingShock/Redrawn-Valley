What it is#

Shade equity is equal access to the cooling benefits of tree cover.[1] The idea is that shade is not a luxury: on a hot day it is the difference between a walk to the bus that is tolerable and one that is not, and it is spread unevenly.

What the evidence shows#

In Los Angeles, TreePeople’s canopy report found that much of the city’s canopy is “not where the people are,” and that some residents live virtually in a forest while many others live in virtually treeless environments.[2] The national picture is similar. The National Equity Atlas reports that across racial and ethnic groups in urban areas, people living below the federal poverty level have lower tree canopy coverage than those above it.[3] American Forests built the Tree Equity Score to prioritize investment where need is greatest.[4]

A 2025 study modeled street trees in one low-income and one high-income Los Angeles neighborhood under two sets of spacing guidelines. Structural differences such as parcel size, intersection density and street width limited how many trees each neighborhood could host. Looser guidelines narrowed the gap in tree numbers, but substantial gaps remained in tree stature, a proxy for canopy. The authors conclude that shade equity may be hard to fix by changing guidelines alone.[5]

Three kinds of fairness#

The Union of Concerned Scientists argues that growing shade should account for more than where trees physically stand. Distributional equity is the map of who has trees. Recognitional equity means understanding the conditions and root causes, and engaging residents about their own goals and concerns. Procedural equity means transparent decisions and fair access to them.[1]

I take that last point seriously for my own work. My studies are drawn from aerial imagery and public research, and they are concept studies. They are not a substitute for asking the people who use a place what they need from it.

Limits of this entry

The UCS piece is an advocacy-science blog post, and I read the Tree Equity Score and National Equity Atlas pages only as summaries. I have not checked the Valley’s shade distribution myself. The Messier study covers two neighborhoods, so I treat it as a case, not a rule.
