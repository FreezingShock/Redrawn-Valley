What it is#

Permeable paving is a family of surfaces that let rain and snowmelt seep through to underlying layers of soil and gravel. The EPA lists pervious asphalt, pervious concrete, interlocking pavers and plastic grid pavers. Besides cutting runoff, permeable pavements can filter pollutants and, by reducing the need for some conventional drainage features, reduce construction costs for residential and commercial development.[1]

Where I have seen it used#

The Elmer Avenue retrofit in Sun Valley installed 63 square feet of permeable concrete and 1,560 square feet of permeable pavers, alongside its bioswales.[2]

Heat is a separate question#

The EPA groups permeable surfaces under “cool pavements” because they can stay cooler by letting water evaporate.[3] But the cooling depends on moisture, and in a dry Valley summer I do not know how much of it is there. I treat permeable paving as a water measure first and a possible heat bonus second. For surface heat, see cool pavement.

A design judgment, labeled#

In my Topanga Willows study I put pavers under the parked stalls but keep asphalt in the drive aisles. That is my judgment about turning loads, not a rule I found in a source,[4] and it is the kind of detail an engineer would have to confirm. Permeable systems also need upkeep to stay open, and I have no cost or maintenance figures for Los Angeles.
