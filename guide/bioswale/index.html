What it is#

A bioswale is a shallow planted channel or bed that takes in runoff from a road, roof or lot and lets it soak into the ground. It is a close relative of the rain garden. The EPA describes a rain garden as a depressed area in the landscape that collects rain from a roof, driveway or street and lets it soak in, planted with grasses and flowering perennials. It can also filter pollutants and shelter wildlife. More complex versions with drainage systems and amended soils are often called bioretention.[1]

Case: Elmer Avenue#

Elmer Avenue in Sun Valley was a street with no pedestrian or stormwater infrastructure and frequent flooding. The retrofit captures and filters runoff from a 40-acre area.[2] The project reports:

  • 24 bioswales that together can capture and treat 115,000 gallons of runoff and add 1,728 square feet of planting.
  • Up to 5.4 million gallons of stormwater infiltrated per year, and reduced lead, copper and suspended solids concentrations by 60%, 33% and 18%.
  • 23 native trees along the sidewalks.
  • Residents satisfied with the block’s walkability rising from under 2% of survey respondents in 2006 to 92% in 2011.

Those are figures reported by the project’s case study brief, not independent audits. The survey is also small and self-reported.[2]

Applying it to a parking lot#

In my Topanga Willows study I turn the strip where car noses overhang at the edge of a lot into a swale, with curb cuts that let runoff in.[3] That is a concept, drawn from a street-scale precedent: Elmer Avenue is a residential street, not a retail lot, and I do not know how the soil under any Woodland Hills lot drains. A percolation test would come before any design is real.
