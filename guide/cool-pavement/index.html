What it is#

The EPA uses cool pavement for paving materials that reflect more solar energy, enhance water evaporation, or have otherwise been modified to stay cooler than conventional pavements. It can be made with ordinary asphalt and concrete, with coatings, or with grass paving.[1] A pilot study in Arizona found conventional paving such as asphalt reaching surface temperatures up to 152°F at midday, while cool pavements stayed 10 to 16°F cooler.[1]

Los Angeles’s experience#

Los Angeles has spent millions installing “cool” pavement on more than 175 lane miles across the city.[2] But a UCLA study discussed by NBC Los Angeles says more research is needed to know the real benefits: cool pavement may lower surface temperatures yet feel hotter to a person, in part because the effect of sunlight is increased as heat re-radiates from the pavement.[2]

I read that as a lesson in what to measure. Surface temperature is easy to read with a thermometer; comfort depends on air temperature, wind, humidity and radiation from every surface around a person.

My working rule#

This is my design judgment, not a sourced finding: shade first, and use reflective coatings only where people are not standing, such as drive aisles. My Topanga Willows study follows that order.[3]
