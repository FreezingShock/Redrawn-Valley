Case study Woodland Hills, Los Angeles

Ventura & Topanga: one small lot could turn a crossing into a place to stop

A 5,437-square-foot building on 0.22 acre at 22001 Ventura Boulevard, with food and groceries already a few minutes away on foot. What would the corner itself need to be somewhere people choose to stay? Six drawn moves.

In short 5 points

  1. People already walk here. Ten places to eat are within 0.16 mile, and two grocers, Ralphs and Sprouts, are inside a quarter mile.[1] The open question is where they would sit.
  2. The lot is tight. The building takes about 57 percent of a 0.22-acre lot (my arithmetic). At one stall per 250 square feet the Specific Plan asks for 22 stalls,[2] which the paving probably cannot hold on its own.
  3. Six moves. Four street trees, a plaza cut from the corner, four shops in place of one, two canopy trees in the paving, a shorter and safer crossing, and a bus shelter with bike racks.
  4. The plaza costs about a tenth of the building. It removes 528 square feet, 9.7 percent of the floor area, and trims the stalls the plan asks for from 21.7 to 19.6. The two canopy trees take about two real stalls.
  5. No revenue claim, and every drawing is an estimate. I found no measured sales effect for plazas or shade at a center this small, and I expect any dimension to be off by 10 percent or more.

Estimates are labeled wherever they appear. What this study does not know is in section 10.

What is real here

The address, building size, lot area, year built, traffic counts and the distances to nearby food and groceries all come from one broker listing.[1] The parking ratio, sidewalk clearances, bus shelter width and head-start signal timing come from City, FHWA and Metro documents. The heat and tree research is cited in section 04.

What is estimated

Everything drawn. The lot is drawn as 100 by 96 feet because that has the right area, and the real lot is probably not a rectangle. The building is 78 by 70 feet (5,460 sq ft against the listing’s 5,437), the sidewalks are 10 feet, and the doors, trees, plaza and shops are my proposal.

Expect any dimension to be off by 10 percent or more. I could not reach the City’s zoning map or the County Assessor from where I worked, so zoning and parcel number are not verified (see section 10).

Part 1 of 4The siteWhat is there and what is around it

01   The site

A building in the middle of its paving, on a corner of two 40,000-car streets

The lot is about 9,600 square feet. Slightly more than half of it is building.

The site is 22001 Ventura Boulevard, a freestanding retail building of 5,437 square feet on a 0.22-acre lot, built in 1980.[1] It is listed for lease as a whole building, with parking on its side and rear.[1]

The listing was last updated on October 27, 2026 and says the space is available immediately, with a sublease running through April 30, 2032.[1] I treat the building as open to change, but I do not know who holds the lease or what the owner wants, and I did not contact anyone.

Half an acre would be 21,780 square feet, so 0.22 acre is about 9,600. If the building is one story, which the listing does not say, it covers about 57 percent of the lot (5,437 divided by 9,583). That leaves roughly 4,100 square feet for parking, walkways and landscape. Fig. 1, in section 05, starts from this plan and adds one move at a time.

The listing gives traffic counts of 41,000 vehicles a day on Ventura and 40,000 on Topanga Canyon.[1] Those are a broker’s figures, I have not matched them to a City or Caltrans count, and a car that turns here is counted on both streets. I use them only for scale: this is a corner people pass at speed far more often than they stop at it.

What the listing cannot tell me is which direction each place lies, whether the walk to it is pleasant, or what the corner offers once you get there. It also lists only some places. I read this as evidence that the foot traffic exists, not as a count of it. I found no public plaza or park in that list within a quarter mile, but that is the listing’s list, not a survey.

Part 2 of 4The evidenceThe rules that bind it and what has been measured elsewhere

03   The rules

At today’s parking ratio the lot cannot hold the stalls this building would need

That makes every stall a trade, and it shapes every move in this study.

Ventura Boulevard has its own set of rules, the Ventura-Cahuenga Boulevard Corridor Specific Plan, which covers the Woodland Hills stretch of the street. Where it is stricter than the general City code it prevails.[2] For commercial uses other than offices it asks for at least one parking space per 250 square feet of floor area.[2] For 5,437 square feet that is 21.7, so 22 stalls.

Here is why that matters. A standard stall is about 9 by 18 feet, which is my assumption, so 22 stalls need at least 3,564 square feet for the stalls alone, out of the roughly 4,100 square feet of paving. That would leave about 540 square feet for a drive aisle, and a drive aisle alone needs more.

So I do not think the lot meets the ratio by itself today. It may rely on the building’s age, on shared parking, or on a rule I could not check. I could not read the lot’s parcel record, so I do not know which, and section 10 lists it as unknown.

On the public sidewalk, the City’s sidewalk dining policy asks for at least 4 feet between tables and fixed things like poles and tree wells, with 5-by-5-foot passing areas every 200 feet, and a barrier where less than 5 feet is left.[3] Other sidewalk encroachments must keep a clear pedestrian route at least 5 feet wide.[4]

A bus shelter needs an alighting area at least 8 feet deep.[7] And Topanga Canyon Boulevard is a state route here (State Route 27), so changes at the crossing may need Caltrans as well as the City. I took that from Wikipedia and have not confirmed it for this intersection.[9]

04   The evidence

What measured studies say about heat, shade and stopping

I looked for measured results. I did not find a measured sales effect for plazas or shade at a center this small, so this study makes no revenue claim.

The heat is real

121°F was recorded at Pierce College in Woodland Hills on Sept. 6, 2020, the highest official reading in Los Angeles County history.[10][11]

Local record

Shade does the most

The EPA reports shaded surfaces can be 20 to 45°F cooler than the same surface in the sun at peak, and trees cool the air too.[12]

EPA

A shaded lot is cooler

In a shopping-center lot in Davis, California, air in the shaded half ran 1 to 2°C cooler and parked cars’ fuel tanks 2 to 4°C cooler.[13]

Peer reviewed

Soil decides the tree

At Walt Disney World, researchers checked 1,127 parking-lot trees. Every tree with at least 1,500 cubic feet of soil was in good condition.[14] Virginia Tech guidance is 2 to 3 cubic feet of soil per square foot of mature canopy.[15]

Field study

A head start for walkers

A leading pedestrian interval lets people enter the crosswalk 3 to 7 seconds before vehicles get a green, so they are already in view of turning drivers.[8]

FHWA

The City’s landscape guidelines ask for at least 50 square feet of open soil at each trunk.[16] I use that as the floor, not the goal: the Virginia Tech number says a tree with a 30-foot canopy, which covers about 700 square feet, wants 1,400 to 2,100 cubic feet of soil (my arithmetic from [15]). Planting a tree and getting shade are different things, and the soil is what turns one into the other.

Part 3 of 4The designSix moves, drawn and counted

05   Six moves

Give up one tenth of the building and gain a corner

Six moves, added one at a time. Scroll: the plan changes with each step.

Today

A building in the middle of its paving

About 5,437 square feet of building, with paving wrapped around two sides. The accent tint is where I expect the sun to bake the surface. Everything here is my estimate.

Move 1

Four street trees

Two along Ventura and two along Topanga, about 40 feet apart. They shade the walk first, which is what people use before they ever enter the lot. They need City approval, and I did not check utilities.

Move 2

Cut the corner for a plaza

Remove a 24-by-22-foot corner of the building: 528 square feet, 9.7 percent of its floor area. It becomes a paved plaza with one tree and a place to sit, on private land so the sidewalk stays clear. This is the one big cost in the study.

Move 3

One building becomes four shops

Units of about 950 to 1,820 square feet, each with a door on a street or on the plaza, and a 4-foot awning over each door. I have not seen the structure, so the party walls are a guess about where it would be easy to divide.

Move 4

Two canopy trees in the paving

Each replaces one stall (about 160 square feet at 9 by 18) with a planting island. The island is drawn at 50 square feet, the City minimum at the trunk, with soil continuing under the paving, because a bare minimum does not grow a canopy.

Move 5

A shorter, safer crossing

Painted crosswalks, curb extensions at the corner, and a signal that lets people on foot go 3 to 7 seconds early.[8] This happens outside the lot, so it is the City’s call, and maybe Caltrans’ on Topanga.

Move 6

A bus shelter and bike racks

Metro Line 150 runs along Ventura and Topanga Canyon[5] and a trip page shows buses about every 20 minutes.[6] A shelter needs an 8-foot clear area.[7] I do not know where the stop sits today, so the shelter is a placeholder for Metro to place.

The honest summary is a trade. The plaza costs about 10 percent of the floor area and, at the Specific Plan’s ratio, cuts the stalls the building would need by about 2. The canopy trees remove about 2 physical stalls. Whether that nets out depends on how many stalls the lot really has and how the current use meets the ratio, and I could not find either.

A lighter version skips the canopy trees and keeps every stall. It gives up the shade over the paving but loses no parking. Unit areas come from the drawn dimensions, so they add to 4,932 square feet against the 4,909 above (the drawing is a little larger than the listing).

07   The section

A 10-foot sidewalk fits a clear walk or tables, not both

So the seating goes in the plaza, on private land.

I assumed a 10-foot sidewalk, which I did not measure. The City asks for a clear route at least 5 feet wide.[4] That leaves 5 feet, and a street tree, a bench and a table zone all want some of it. Tables would also trigger the 4-foot clearance and barrier rules for sidewalk dining.[3]

The section shows the answer I chose: a 4-foot awning over the walk, trees and a bench in the outer 5 feet, and every table in the plaza behind the property line. If the real sidewalk is wider the picture gets easier. If it is narrower, the trees move into the lot.

08   The crossing, looped

Five seconds is the difference between sharing the crosswalk and being past the turn

Both panels loop every 12 seconds. Watch the dotted circle.

FHWA describes a leading pedestrian interval as giving people 3 to 7 seconds in the crosswalk before vehicles get a green.[8] I drew 5, the midpoint. These panels show the idea only. They are not a signal-timing plan, the geometry is generic, and I did not count real turning cars. Under reduced-motion settings the walker stands still halfway.

09   Measuring it

Five numbers I would take before and after

People who stop

Count people seated or standing for more than two minutes at the corner, every 15 minutes, across one Saturday and one weekday.

people

Surface temperature

An infrared thermometer at the same six spots at 3 pm: the plaza, both sidewalks, and three in the paving.

°F

Real stalls and use

Count the stalls on the lot and how many are taken at noon and 6 pm for a week. This replaces my guess and shows what a stall is worth.

stalls

Crossing conflicts

Two peak hours at the corner with a notebook: how often a turning car and a person on foot reach the same spot together.

events

Doors in use

How many of the proposed doors are open at 11 am, noon and 5 pm, and what each unit sells.

10   Limits

What this study does not know

The parcel number, zoning and owner. The City’s ZIMAS map and the County Assessor would not load for me, so lot area, size and year built come only from a broker listing.[1] The address-to-corner match rests on that listing, and ZIMAS would settle it.

Which quadrant of the intersection the lot sits in and how it is oriented to north. I did not set either, so this study makes no claim about which side faces the afternoon sun.

The lot’s real shape and dimensions, the sidewalk widths, the building’s stories and structure, and where its doors are today.

The real stall count, and how the current use meets the 1-per-250 ratio. The Specific Plan’s maps would also say whether any extra pedestrian-area rules apply to this lot.

Whether Caltrans controls the Topanga crossing, where the bus stop sits, and what any of the street changes would be allowed to do.

The lease, the owner’s plans, utilities, easements, fire access and trash routes, any of which can move a tree or a wall.

What the four other corners look like. They are drawn as blocks marked “not studied,” and a district plan would start there.

Cost, and whether smaller shops would earn enough to carry the lost floor area. I found no measured number I could defend, so I give none.
