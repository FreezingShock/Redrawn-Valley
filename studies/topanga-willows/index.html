Case studyWoodland Hills, Los Angeles

Topanga Willows: shade for a block that faces the afternoon sun

How one small shopping center on Topanga Canyon Boulevard, between Providencia Street and Dumetz Road, could get cooler, greener and worth staying at, using trees, soil and rain.

Part 1 of 4The siteWhat is there, and why it is hot
01   The site

A building on the sidewalk, a parking lot behind it

Topanga Willows fills the east side of Topanga Canyon Boulevard for one block, from Providencia Street down to Dumetz Road.

The building is one long single-story bar, roughly 210 feet by 50, set right on the sidewalk with red awnings on both faces.[31] The owner lists it at 9,809 square feet.[1] The map shows a nail salon, a cleaner, a quilt shop, The Yoga Center and a pizza place; the owner also lists Starbucks and Yogis Grill.[1][31]

All the parking is behind the building, on the east: four rows of angled stalls on two one-way aisles, about 68 stalls, with driveways on Providencia and on Dumetz. Houses back onto the east edge. The whole lot has one small tree in it.[31]

The owner counts about 30,463 vehicles a day on Topanga Canyon and 11,457 on Dumetz, with 114,413 people living within three miles.[1] A second listing gives 36,641 and 9,424, so I treat both as rough.[2]

The orientation is the whole story. Because the building sits on the west side of its lot, it shades only the first row of stalls late in the day. The rest of the lot is in the sun from morning on. And the side people actually walk along, the storefronts on Topanga Canyon, faces west into the afternoon sun.

One constraint: Topanga Canyon Boulevard is State Route 27, a Caltrans road.[29] The planted strip between the curb and the sidewalk is public right-of-way, so anything there is a request to the agencies, not something the owner can just build.

02   The problem

The hottest official reading in county history was taken a few miles from here

Woodland Hills is one of the hottest parts of Los Angeles. A parking lot makes that worse in exactly the place people have to walk.

121°F

Recorded at Pierce College in Woodland Hills on Sept. 6, 2020. It is the highest temperature at an official station in Los Angeles County. The old record, 119°F in 2006, was also Woodland Hills.[3][4]

120–150°F

Peak summer surface temperature of conventional paving, according to the EPA.[5]

1,500+

Extra emergency room visits in Los Angeles County on a hot day, from UCLA data reported by NBC Los Angeles.[9]

NASA researchers found that heatwaves in Southern California got more frequent, more intense and longer from 1950 to 2020, with the biggest change in inland urban areas, which sit far from sea breezes and are covered in surfaces that store heat.[4] Tree canopy covers about 25 percent of the City of Los Angeles, and it is spread unevenly.[8] I do not have a canopy number for this block. Distance to Pierce College is my estimate; check it on a map.

03   The evidence

What the research says works

I looked for measured results, not claims. Six findings shaped the design.

Shade is the strongest tool. Shaded surfaces can be far cooler than the same material in the sun.[6]

20 to 45°F cooler at peak

Trees also cool the air by releasing water through their leaves.[6]

2 to 9°F lower peak summer temperatures

In a shopping center lot in Davis, California, the shaded half had cooler air, and cars parked there had cooler fuel tanks.[11]

Air 1 to 2°C cooler; fuel tanks 2 to 4°C cooler

Urban trees catch rain before it reaches the pavement.[7]

15 to 27 percent of annual rainfall absorbed

Permeable paving has strong evidence behind it for cutting runoff and pollutants.[23]

Rated “strong evidence”

A Los Angeles study modeled more tree cover and more reflective surfaces and found fewer heat-related emergency room visits.[10]

Peer reviewed, 2024

The catch: planting a tree is not the same as getting shade

Sacramento has required 50 percent shade over parking lots since 1983, and the Forest Service went back to check whether lots were meeting it.[12] A survey in Rocklin, California found that fewer than 3 percent of parking spaces were at least half shaded, that most lot trees were well below mature size, and that soil conditions were the thing to fix.[13] At Walt Disney World, researchers checked 1,127 parking lot trees. Every tree with at least 1,500 cubic feet of soil was in good condition.[14] That is why section 07 is about soil.

04   Precedents

Two projects in the Valley, and one warning

1

Elmer Avenue, Sun Valley

A residential block rebuilt in 2010 with bioswales, permeable pavers, 23 native trees and infiltration galleries under the street.[19] It can infiltrate up to 5.4 million gallons of stormwater a year, and residents who were satisfied with the block’s walkability went from under 2 percent to 92 percent.[20] Lesson: water and comfort can be solved by the same planting.

2

Cool pavement, Pacoima

A reflective coating over ten blocks lowered surface temperatures by 10°F or more and air temperature by up to 3.5°F on extreme days.[21] Lesson: coatings help the surface, so they belong on drive aisles.

3

The warning

A UCLA study covered by NBC Los Angeles found reflective pavement can make a person standing on it feel hotter, because the sunlight bounces back up.[22] Lesson: shade first. Reflective coating only where people do not stand.

The rows, aisles and driveways stay where they are. Scroll and the plan builds up from today to the full proposal.

Today

Asphalt, four rows, one tree

About 68 angled stalls. The building shades the first row late in the day; everything else is in the sun.[31]

Stage 1: canopy

A row of trees down the middle

Eight trees in the center double row: an island at each end, and six diamond planters cut into the triangles that angled stalls leave between them. The diamonds cost no parking.[17]

Stage 2: east edge

A planted edge toward the houses

The strip where car noses overhang becomes a swale with a hedge and seven trees. Curb cuts let runoff in, and vines cover the fence.[18]

Stage 3: pavers

Permeable paving in every stall

Parked cars sit on pavers that let water through to the soil below. Aisles stay asphalt because they take the turning loads.[23]

Stage 4: patio

The three stalls at the south end of the storefront row join the corner paving to make one patio, with two small trees and a planter wall toward the cars.

Stage 5: walk

One striped crossing through the middle island, planters along the back walk, and a reflective coating on the aisles only, for the reason in section 04.[21][22]

All seven moves

A real parkway on Topanga Canyon

Native grasses replace the lawn strip and two street trees fill the gaps, so the west-facing sidewalk gets afternoon shade. This one needs the City and Caltrans.[29]

68 → 61

Stalls, before and after. Seven are given up: three for the patio and four for the end islands. The code lets some required car spaces be swapped for bike parking at four bikes per car space.[28]

17

New trees on the property. That is one for every four existing stalls, the ratio Los Angeles asks of new lots, along with half the stall area shaded after ten years.[17]

11,200 gal

Seven stalls is about a tenth of the parking, and the owner would be right to ask whether the tenants can spare them. A lighter version skips the end islands and gives up only the three patio stalls.

07   The detail that decides it

Soil, not trees, is the hard part

A tree in a small cutout in compacted fill stays small for life. The roots need loose soil, and in a parking lot the only place to find it is under the paving.

Guidance from Virginia Tech is 2 to 3 cubic feet of soil for every square foot of mature canopy.[15] For a tree with a 30-foot canopy that works out to about 1,400 cubic feet.[16]

Los Angeles requires at least 50 square feet of open soil at each trunk, 5 feet wide at minimum, and a 24-inch box tree at planting.[17] A diamond planter between angled stalls is smaller than that at the surface, which is exactly why the soil has to continue under the pavers in soil cells.

The pavers and the soil do two jobs at once: they water the trees with runoff, and they count toward the stormwater volume.

08   Planting

Five trees, each with a job

All five are California natives. The name of the center asks for willows, and it turns out a real one grows wild a few miles away.

Coast live oak

Quercus agrifolia
Center row, 7

Evergreen, so the shade is there in every month. It is the oak the hills on the south side of the Valley are known for and can reach 80 feet.[24]

Valley oak

Quercus lobata
North island, 1

The big deciduous oak. One, at the Providencia end, where it has the most room.[24]

Western sycamore

Platanus racemosa
East swale, 5

A streamside tree that has been seen thriving in the hotter parts of the Valley. It goes where the water collects.[24][27]

Arroyo willow

Salix lasiolepis
Swale low end, 2

The namesake. A true willow recorded in the Sepulveda Basin. It needs regular water, so it only goes at the lowest, wettest end of the swale.[24][25]

Desert willow

Chilopsis linearis
Patio, 2

Not a true willow, but named for its narrow leaves, and native to desert washes. Small enough to sit under.[26]

Below the trees: a clipped native hedge and grasses in the swale, shrubs in the end islands, planters along the back walk, and native grasses in the parkway in place of lawn. The flowering layer borrows from the Elmer Avenue plant list: autumn sage, yarrow, penstemon and blue-eyed grass.[30] Not verified: ten-year canopy size for each species in this soil, and which end of the swale is actually lowest. An arborist and a site visit should settle both.

Part 4 of 4DeliveryHow it could happen and what is still unknown
10   Phasing

What could happen first

1

Prove it

Close the three patio stalls with planters and seating for a summer. Measure whether people stay. Nothing is demolished.

2

Plant it

Cut the diamonds and end islands, install soil cells, and plant the center row and the east edge in fall so the trees root before the heat.

3

Plumb it

Lay the pavers, open the curb cuts into the swale, coat the aisles, and take the parkway request to the City and Caltrans.

No cost estimate is included. I did not find numbers I could defend for this site.

11   Measuring it

Four numbers I would take before and after

Surface temperature

An infrared thermometer on the same six spots at 3 pm: sidewalk, patio, and four in the lot.

Stall shade

Percent of stall area in shade at solar noon on June 21, the City’s own test.

Staying

People seated outside, counted every 15 minutes across one Saturday.

Runoff

Which driveway water leaves by in a light storm. A phone video is enough, and it tells you where the swale’s low end is.

12   Limits

What this study does not know

Exact dimensions and the exact stall count. Everything is scaled from an aerial photo.

Which way the lot slopes and where its drains are. The swale assumes water can be led to the east edge.

Whether the soil drains well enough to infiltrate. That takes a soils report.

Which unit the coffee shop is in and where its seating is today. I put both at the Dumetz corner.

How many stalls the tenants’ uses legally require, and whether the City’s landscape and stormwater rules would apply to a retrofit this size. I used them as targets.

Utilities, easements, fire access and trash pickup routes, any of which can move a tree.

What the neighbors on the east side think about trees on their fence line.

Cost, and who pays for watering the trees through their first three summers.
