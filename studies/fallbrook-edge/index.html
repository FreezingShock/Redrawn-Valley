Fallbrook Edge: a 55-acre center with 1,770 feet of street frontage, and a case for shading it

Part 1 of 4The siteWhat is there

01   The site

A center that draws 11 million visits a year has a front edge nobody designed for walking

Why I picked the Fallbrook Avenue frontage out of 55 acres.

Fallbrook Center is a 1,120,000 square foot open-air center on the west side of Fallbrook Avenue, anchored by Ralphs, Walmart, Trader Joe’s and Sprouts[1]. The owner’s site plan lists 52 units. Its mobile-location vendor counts 11.1 million visits and 1.2 million visitors a year, and ranks it fifth among shopping centers within 15 miles, behind Westfield Village at Topanga, Northridge Fashion Center, Westfield Topanga and Century City[1]. That is a vendor figure in a leasing brochure, so I read it as a sign of scale, not a precise count. Divided by 365 it is about 30,000 visits a day, my arithmetic.

It began as Fallbrook Square in the 1960s, was enclosed and renamed Fallbrook Mall in 1986, then opened back up as an open-air center in 2003[3][4][5]. The Wikipedia page and the Times disagree on the opening date (1963 versus 1964) and the Times and the Daily News disagree on the year it was built (1964 versus 1966). I only need the decade.

Why this section. A shopping center this size has many places to study. I chose the edge for three reasons. First, it is the part of the property the public owns a view of: 1,770 feet, about a third of a mile, facing a street that is about 100 feet wide. Second, an edge can be redesigned without touching a single store, which makes it a realistic first move for an owner. Third, it is where the parcel lines let me draw something exact instead of guessing, since the frontage is one straight line on the City’s map.

02   The problem

A big center in the Valley sits in the part of Los Angeles where heat is getting worse

NASA researchers found that Southern California heatwaves grew more frequent, more intense and longer from 1950 to 2020, with the biggest change in inland urban areas[7]. West Hills is that kind of place. Tree canopy covers about 25 percent of the City of Los Angeles and is spread unevenly[9]. I do not have a canopy number for this edge, and I have not seen what is planted along it today.

What I can say from the numbers I do have: a center this large is mostly paved, and the walk from the street to a store door crosses a parking field. I am not claiming it is bare. I am proposing a standard for the edge that I can check on a visit: shade over the walk, soil that lets the trees reach full size, and a way in for people on foot.

03   The evidence

Shade works, trees in parking lots usually fail on soil, and a row of stalls costs little

These are the same studies I used in study 01, read there, and used here for the same reasons.

Shade. Shaded surfaces can be far cooler than the same material in the sun, and trees cool the air by releasing water through their leaves[8]. In a shopping center lot in Davis, California, the shaded half had cooler air and the cars parked in it had cooler fuel tanks[10]. A 2024 Los Angeles study linked more tree cover and reflective surfaces to fewer heat-related emergency room visits[18].

Why lot trees fail. Sacramento has required 50 percent shade over parking lots since 1983[11]. A survey in Rocklin, California found fewer than 3 percent of parking spaces at least half shaded and most lot trees well below mature size, with soil as the thing to fix[12]. At Walt Disney World, every one of 1,127 lot trees checked that had at least 1,500 cubic feet of soil was in good condition[13]. Virginia Tech guidance is 2 to 3 cubic feet of soil per square foot of mature canopy[14], about 1,400 cubic feet for a 30-foot canopy[15].

Water. Los Angeles uses 0.75 inch of rain as its design storm, and its sample calculations show how much a swale and paving must hold[17]. I did not run that calculation here because I do not have the paved area of the edge.

Rules. Los Angeles asks for at least 50 square feet of open soil at each trunk, 5 feet wide at minimum, and a 24-inch box tree at planting[16]. I did not look up zoning for these parcels. Opening ZIMAS was blocked from my workspace, so each APN’s zone, height limit and any overlay is unverified. The Community Plan for this area dates from 1999 and the City says an update is underway[6].

04   The proposal

A strip of soil, a row of trees and a walk, for at most 196 stalls

Four moves, shown on the frontage of one parcel and then applied to the whole edge.

I drew the proposal on the stretch in front of parcel 012 (6701 N Fallbrook Ave), a 486-foot piece of the edge, because it is long enough to show the rhythm and short enough to read. The plan is rotated so the frontage runs left to right with north at the right. Scroll through the stages or tap them.

Today

The one thing I know: where the line is

The parcel line and the 100-foot avenue are real. The single row of 54 nine-foot stalls is my assumption for what sits between the street and the stores, drawn so I can show what the proposal takes.

Move 1

A 10-foot strip of soil

The outer 18 feet, the stall row I assume is there, gives way to an 8-foot walk and a 10-foot strip. The strip becomes a shallow swale over a continuous rooting zone, so rain from the edge soaks in and roots have room.

Move 2

One tree every 30 feet

That is 16 trees on this stretch and 59 along the whole 1,770 feet. At a 30-foot mature canopy, neighbors touch, so the walk gets a continuous roof.

Move 3

An 8-foot walk and curb cuts

The walk runs the full length beside the strip. Curb cuts between trees let runoff from the lot slope into the swale.

Move 4

Seats and a way in

Benches every 120 feet, and a marked walk across the stall row to the stores, so someone arriving on foot does not have to weave between cars.

What it costs. At 9 feet a stall, a full row along 1,770 feet is 196 stalls (1,770 ÷ 9, rounded down). If the center really has about 5,300 stalls, an older figure from Wikipedia that I could not verify[3], that is 3.7 percent. It is an upper bound: pads, drive entries and existing planting along the edge would reduce the stalls I would actually take, and the entries add a few more back. On parcel 012 the same math gives 54 stalls.

What it buys. Each tree gets a 10-foot strip plus an 8-foot walk, 30 feet along: 18 × 30 × 3 feet of soil is 1,620 cubic feet, a little over the 1,400 cubic feet a 30-foot canopy needs[15]. That is the number that has to hold, so the soil runs under the walk as well as the strip.

Decisions I made, and why.

  • Edge, not interior. The edge can change without moving a store or a drive aisle, and it is the one place the public sees from the street.
  • Strip next to the walk. Trees in the lot have to share soil with paving. Soil under the walk and strip gives each tree more room than a lot island would.
  • 30-foot spacing. It matches a 30-foot canopy, so the shade is continuous, and it sets the tree count at 59.
  • Street parkway trees left to the City. The 6-foot parkway in Fig. 3 is in the public right of way. I drew it, but it needs the City.
  • Pads left alone. Where a store or pad already faces the avenue, the strip narrows or stops. The count is the maximum.

06   Limits

What this study does not know

  • What is on the ground. I had no aerial imagery and did not visit. The existing stall row and edge conditions are assumed. A site visit and an aerial would replace the assumption with counts.
  • Zoning and rules. I could not open ZIMAS. Look up each APN there to confirm zone, height district and overlays before relying on anything here.
  • The parcel layer is the 2015 tax roll[2]. Lot lines may have changed, and parcels I did not find would raise the 55-acre total.
  • Conflicting sources. Size is 880,000 sq ft on Wikipedia[3] and 1,120,000 in the owner’s brochure[1]; I used the owner’s. The 5,300 stalls is from Wikipedia and unverified.
  • Utilities, fire access and easements along the edge are unknown. Any of them can move a tree or a swale.
  • Transit. I did not check bus stops on Fallbrook Avenue. If there are stops, the benches should go there first.
  • Owner and City. The owner would have to agree, and the parkway and any curb work are the City’s.
  • Species and ten-year canopy size in this soil need an arborist.
