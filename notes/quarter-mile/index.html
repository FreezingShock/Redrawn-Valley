A quarter mile is not the same walk in August

Transit planners measure how far people will walk. In a place that can hit 121 degrees, the better question is how much of that distance is in the sun.

By Nate AndersonNov. 20, 20263 min read4 sourcesNote 5 of 5

In short 4 points

  1. Planners say people will walk a quarter to a half mile. I could not trace that rule to one original study.1 One study found people will walk much farther than the rule suggests.3
  2. A quarter mile is 1,320 feet, about five minutes at 3 mph. The pace is my assumption, so five minutes is an estimate.
  3. Distance is the same, the walk is not. On a day near the Valley's 121°F record,4 five minutes in the sun costs more than five in April.
  4. I want to add a number, the shaded share of a route. It is my proposal, not a standard: the fraction of a route in shade at a stated hour. I have not collected any yet.

What I could not verify is listed at the end of the note, before the sources.

01The rule of thumb

Ask a transit planner how far people will walk to a bus stop and you get a familiar answer: about a quarter to a half mile, or five to fifteen minutes.1 Agencies write the number into their guidelines. The Boston region’s metropolitan planning organization, for example, describes a coverage guideline that gives residents access within a one-quarter-mile walk of transit in areas with more than 5,000 people per square mile.2

I should be honest about the weakness of my sourcing here. The five-to-fifteen-minute rule is repeated across planning documents, and I could not trace it back to one original study. It reads like folk knowledge that got written down. That does not make it wrong, but it does mean I should not lean on its exact figures.

02What a quarter mile is

The distance itself is simple. A mile is 5,280 feet, so a quarter mile is 1,320 feet. If I assume a walking pace of 3 miles per hour, which is a round number I am choosing and not a measured Valley pace, that is about 4.4 feet per second, and 1,320 feet takes 300 seconds: five minutes.

Five minutes is nothing on a mild day. The problem is that the planning rule describes the walk as if every five minutes costs the same, and in the Valley they do not.

03Distance is not the whole cost

Research on how far people actually walk to transit suggests the rule of thumb is not a hard limit anyway. One study of trip distance and walking to reach public transit concluded that the distance people are willing to walk appears to be much higher than the frequently cited rules of thumb of 0.25 to 0.5 miles.3 I have read only the abstract, so I cannot say how well it applies to Los Angeles.

The distance is the same. The walk is not.

Nate Anderson, this note

That gap is why I do not think distance alone describes a walk. If willingness depends on the walk being tolerable, then shade, seating and surface can change who is willing to make it. I also know, from the first note in this series, that the Valley’s official record is 121°F.4 On a day near that, five minutes at the same pace is not the same experience as five minutes in April.

04A measure I want to try

I want to add one number to my studies. I will call it the shaded share of a route. It is mine, and it is not an established standard, so read it as a proposal. The steps are simple enough that anyone could repeat them:

  • Pick a route someone really takes, such as bus stop to storefront door.
  • Walk it at a stated hour on a sunny day and mark each 100 feet as mostly shaded or mostly in sun.
  • Report the shaded share as a fraction of the route, with the date, hour and orientation next to it.
  • Draw the proposal and estimate the new share, labeled as an estimate.

It is crude. Shade moves with the sun, and a pass at 3 p.m. says nothing about 11 a.m. But a crude, repeatable number beats a missing one, and it connects the distance people cite to the thing they feel. I have not collected any of this yet. Topanga Willows is the first place I would try it, because its walkway faces west.

What I could not verify

  • The origin of the five-to-fifteen-minute rule, and any Los Angeles-specific walking-distance data.
  • The 3 mph pace is an assumption, so the five-minute figure is an estimate.
  • I read only the abstract of the transit-walking study, and the Boston guideline is one region’s policy, not a national standard.
