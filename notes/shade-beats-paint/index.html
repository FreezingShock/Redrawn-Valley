Shade beats paint, and Pacoima shows why

Los Angeles coated 700,000 square feet of asphalt in a reflective surface and measured the result. The numbers are real. They also answer a smaller question than the one I care about.

By NathanNov. 20, 20263 min read2 sourcesNote 2 of 5

01What was tested

In the summer of 2022, more than 700,000 square feet of dark asphalt in Pacoima, a neighborhood in the northeast San Fernando Valley, was covered with a solar-reflective coating. The project was a partnership between the local nonprofit Climate Resolve and the roofing and waterproofing manufacturer GAF, which supplied the coating.1 Haider Taha, an atmospheric modeler, studied it for a year, and the results were published in the journal Environmental Research Communications.1

One thing to say plainly: GAF funded the study. The reporting says the company had no role in its design, data collection, modeling or analysis, and the paper was peer reviewed.1 I think that is worth stating because the coating maker also benefits when the coating works. I have not read the paper itself, only coverage of it, so I cannot check the method myself.

02What it found

During an extreme heat event, the coated area had air temperatures as much as 3.5°F cooler than the adjacent neighborhood. On sunny days the reduction was up to 2.1°F, and on summer nights up to 0.5°F. The coating also lowered surface temperatures by up to around 10°F.1 An earlier city report on a Los Angeles cool pavement installation found it averaged 12.8°F cooler than nearby uncoated black asphalt.2

Those are real cooling effects, and I do not want to wave them away. A surface that is 10°F cooler stores and re-radiates less heat after sunset, which is probably why the biggest air-temperature effect showed up during the extreme heat event rather than on ordinary days. That last part is my guess at the mechanism, not something the study claims in the coverage I read.

03The catch is what you measure

Almost every number above is about the air or the pavement. Neither is a person. The coverage notes that some researchers have found reflected sunlight can raise how hot pedestrians feel, and Taha said his study indicated an “improvement in thermal sensation.” He also said mean radiant temperature, a measure of thermal comfort, “goes up and down. It’s not always cooler,” and that “The overwhelming effect is the cooling.”1 Both statements can be true. A reflective street can cool the neighborhood and still push some light onto the person walking on it.

“No change in surface will protect the body from heat as effectively as preventing sunlight from hitting the body in the first place.”

V. Kelly Turner, UCLA Luskin Center for Innovation, quoted by Smart Cities Dive [1]

Turner, associate director of UCLA’s Luskin Center for Innovation, wrote in an email to the reporter that shade can cool people by up to 30°C, or 54°F, in hot, dry environments, and that reflective pavement does not have a large effect on air temperature unless it is deployed widely, and even then the benefit is moderate.1 Note that her 54-degree figure is a different kind of number from the bars above: it describes how much cooler a person can be made, not how much cooler the street gets. I kept them out of the same chart for that reason.

04Where paint still makes sense

I do not read this as “never coat pavement.” Turner herself says reflective pavement is an appropriate choice where it is hard to replace heat-absorbing surfaces with something like vegetation, and where the goal is to reduce what the surface materials add to the area’s heat. She calls it one tool among many for the regional heat island, not a substitute for protecting people from the sun.1

That fits how many lots are laid out. A parking aisle has to stay open for cars. A tree cannot go in the middle of it. A coating could, in principle, do some work there, and shade trees go on the edges, in planting strips and between rows. For my own studies I treat it this way, as a proposal and not a result:

  • Shade first: trees, canopies and buildings between the sun and the people.
  • Surface second: where trees physically cannot go, consider a reflective coat, and say what I do not know about its cost and upkeep. I have not priced it.
  • Always say which quantity a claim is about: air, surface or body.

What I could not verify

  • I read the Smart Cities Dive coverage and the snippet of the city report, not the journal paper or the full report.
  • The 12.8°F figure is from one city installation and compares to nearby black asphalt; it is not the same test as Pacoima.
  • I do not have cost, durability or maintenance numbers for the coating, so I make no claim about value for money.
