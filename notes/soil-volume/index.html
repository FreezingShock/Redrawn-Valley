A shade tree is a soil problem first

Everyone agrees the Valley needs more trees. Almost nobody draws the hole they have to grow in. The arithmetic is simple, and it changes what a street can hold.

By NathanNov. 20, 20264 min read4 sourcesNote 3 of 5

01The rule of thumb everyone repeats

If you ask how much soil a big street tree needs, you hear a number: about 1,000 cubic feet. It is a long-repeated rule of thumb. DeepRoot, a company that sells soil-cell systems for street trees, calls it “a helpful rudimentary rule of thumb” and says the industry has moved beyond it.1 A seller of tree-soil products has a reason to say more is better, so I treat that as a source for the existence of the rule and not as proof of the right number.

A neutral reference is the Minnesota Pollution Control Agency’s stormwater manual, which lists example minimum tree soil volumes from different jurisdictions. Among them: 30 cubic meters (1,059 cubic feet) of soil per tree, and 20 cubic meters (706 cubic feet) per tree when the volume is shared between trees.2 I do not know which jurisdictions those two entries belong to, only that they are listed as real requirements.

02A second way to get the same number

The American Society of Landscape Architects offers a different route: trees need about 1.5 to 2 cubic feet of loam soil per square foot of the area under their mature canopy.3 That makes the volume depend on how big the tree gets, which is more honest than one number for every species.

Try it with a tree that has a 30-foot crown. I picked 30 feet as an example, it is not a measured tree. A circle 30 feet across has an area of about 707 square feet (π × 15²). At 1.5 to 2 cubic feet per square foot, that is roughly 1,060 to 1,410 cubic feet of soil. That lands right around the 1,000-to-1,059 figures above, which is the reason I trust the order of magnitude even though I do not trust any single rule to the last foot.

A tree is a bet on how much soil it can reach.

Nathan, this note

The academic version of the idea goes back at least to 1991, when Lindsey and Bassuk published a method for specifying soil volumes to meet the water needs of mature street trees and trees in containers.4 I have read its title and abstract page, not the full paper, so I am citing it for the existence of the approach, not for its numbers.

03The tree well next to it

Now compare that with the hole most sidewalk trees are given. I do not have a measured sample, so I use an assumption and label it: a small tree well of 4 by 4 feet, dug 3 feet deep. That is 48 cubic feet. Against a 1,000-cubic-foot target it is about one twenty-first, and against the 1,059 figure about one twenty-second. Those ratios are my arithmetic on my assumed well.

Spread over 3 feet of depth, 1,000 cubic feet needs about 333 square feet of soil surface, which is roughly 18 feet by 18 feet. The picture matters more than the ratio. A sidewalk planting strip is often only a few feet wide, so the soil a tree needs does not fit under the strip. It has to run under the paving, the parking lot or the road edge, where it competes with everything else buried there.

04How I would check a real strip

If I were sizing soil for a real planting strip, I would work in this order, and I would stop at the first step I could not complete.

  • Measure the strip. Width, length and the clear width left on the walk beside it. The surface area available is the first limit.
  • Find what is below. Request utility information and look for as-built drawings before assuming any depth is free.
  • Pick a tree and its mature crown. Then apply the 1.5 to 2 cubic feet per square foot rule from the landscape architects as a first pass.3
  • Compare with what fits. Multiply the strip’s area by a usable depth. If the answer is far under the target, the choices are a smaller tree, shared soil with a neighbor, or a different location, not a hopeful drawing of a large tree.

The honest result is often “a smaller tree than I wanted.” That is still better shade than a large tree that dies young in a small hole.

05What that does to a plan

This is the part I find most interesting as someone heading toward civil engineering. The tree is the easy line on the drawing. The hard parts are underneath:

  • Utilities. Water, sewer, gas and electric lines already occupy the ground. Before drawing any soil volume I would need a locate and as-built drawings, not an assumption that the ground is empty.
  • Sharing. The 706-cubic-foot shared figure suggests one way out: a continuous soil trench under a row of trees, instead of separate pits. That is my reading of the shared-volume entry, and I have not seen it detailed for Los Angeles.
  • Paving on top. If the soil is under a walk or lot, the paving has to carry loads over it, which is a structural design question, and the reason systems like soil cells exist. I have not engineered that and am not claiming a solution.
  • Width on the surface. The tree and its tree guard cannot narrow the walkway below the accessible minimum. The next note covers that rule.

What I could not verify

  • The 4 × 4 × 3 ft well is my assumption of a small well, not a surveyed one.
  • I do not know the soil-volume requirement in the City of Los Angeles code, and I do not claim one here.
  • The rule-of-thumb figures vary by source and by species; none of these is a design value for a specific tree.
