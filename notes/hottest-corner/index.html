The hottest day on record in L.A. County happened in Woodland Hills

A 121-degree reading makes a headline. For a site study, the better question is what the thermometer can and cannot tell me about the block I am drawing.

By NathanNov. 20, 20265 min read5 sourcesNote 1 of 5

01One reading, and what it cannot tell you

On September 6, 2020, the official weather station in Woodland Hills recorded 121°F. The National Weather Service said it was the hottest temperature ever recorded at an official station in Los Angeles County. The old record was 119°F, set in July 2006.1 The same weekend, Burbank appeared to tie its own all-time record at 114°F.1 The National Weather Service’s Los Angeles office described the Woodland Hills site as the one at Pierce College.2

I started this site with a Woodland Hills sidewalk, so that number is not trivia to me. But one day is one day. A record is rare by definition, and I do not want to open a study by treating it as typical. What it does tell me is the ceiling: this part of the Valley can do that, and anything I draw for it has to survive that kind of day.

Notice what the figure does and does not show. It shows three official readings. It does not show how hot any particular street was, because an official station sits in one spot and a sidewalk across town has different surroundings. Treat the number as a ceiling for the region, not a measurement of your block.

02Heat islands are local, and canopy is what they track

Some of the hottest spots in the Valley are not hot because of the Valley. They are hot because of what is on the ground. A study for the California Energy Commission used a calibrated weather model and observations to map neighborhood-scale heat islands across Los Angeles. Its authors include Haider Taha, who also ran the Pacoima cool pavement analysis I come back to in the next note.3

The sentence in that report that matters most for my work is short. In the San Fernando Valley study area, the largest localized heat islands were associated with the lowest vegetation canopy cover.3 The report’s model also put higher temperatures in the industrial and commercial areas running from near Chatsworth down through Canoga Park and Woodland Hills, and along major roads such as Sherman Way, Parthenia Street and Reseda Boulevard, with cool islands tied to areas of higher vegetation cover.3

“In the San Fernando Valley study area, the largest localized heat islands were associated with lowest vegetation canopy cover.”

Taha and colleagues, California Energy Commission, 2018 [3]

That is one modeled study from 2018, and the report notes that near downtown a different factor, lower albedo, drove the largest local heat islands.3 So I do not read it as “trees fix everything.” I read it as a reason to check canopy on my own block first, because in this part of the city missing canopy is exactly where the heat concentrates.

03Shade is not spread evenly

If canopy matters that much, the next question is where it is. TreePeople’s analysis of Los Angeles tree canopy found that about a fifth (18%) of the city’s tree canopy grows where only 1% of the city’s population lives. That canopy sits in just five census block groups, which together hold under a tenth (8%) of the city’s land, in Pacific Palisades, Los Feliz, Brentwood and Shadow Hills.4 The same analysis lists the areas in highest need of protective canopy: southeast county along the Interstate 110 corridor, the northeast part of the San Fernando Valley, and northern Los Angeles County.4

My reading of that, and it is my reading, not theirs: shade is not missing evenly, it is missing in specific places, and the places are not hard to find. A study that starts from the city average will be wrong about its own block. A study that starts by counting the trees within sight of the site will not.

04Air temperature is not what a body feels

A weather station reports air temperature. A person standing on a sidewalk also takes in sun on their skin and heat given off by the paving and the walls around them. The combined effect is usually described with a measure called mean radiant temperature, which the coverage of the Pacoima cool pavement study calls “a measurement of thermal comfort.”5

This is why a 2-degree change in air temperature can be much less important than whether you are standing in shade. It is also why I try not to quote air temperature alone when I describe a site. The next note works through a real test of that idea in Pacoima.

05A ten-minute check for any block

You do not need a lab to apply this. Before I propose anything for a block, I do a short check, and every step is something a person can repeat from a phone and a sidewalk.

  • Count the crowns. Stand at the middle of the block and count the trees whose canopy actually overhangs the walk, not the trees on the lot behind it. Write the number down, including zero.
  • Note the face. Which way does the walk face? A west-facing walk takes the afternoon sun, a north-facing one mostly does not. Orientation decides which hour is the problem.
  • Look at what is under the feet. Dark asphalt, light concrete, or planting. The Taha report links low albedo to heat islands near downtown, so surface color is a real variable.3
  • Stand in it. Pick a sunny afternoon and stand in the sun for two minutes, then in the nearest shade for two. I have no instrument for this and I am not claiming a number. The point is to notice how large the difference feels before reading anyone’s statistics about it.

None of that replaces data. It does stop me from arriving at a site with a conclusion already decided.

06What I take into the studies

Three habits come out of this, and they are about method more than heat.

  • Write down the orientation and the hour. Topanga Willows’ storefront side faces west, so the afternoon is the worst hour there. A reading from noon would hide that.
  • Look up canopy before proposing trees, and say what the block has today, even if the answer is “one small tree.”
  • Never use a record as if it were typical. Use it as the load the design has to carry, the way an engineer checks a structure against the worst case, not the average day.

What I could not verify

  • I read the energy commission report’s abstract and key findings and some of its results, not all 58 pages.
  • I have taken no temperature readings myself, anywhere. Nothing here is a measurement of my own.
  • I could only see the National Weather Service post as a search excerpt, so I rely on the L.A. Times for the 121-degree figure.
