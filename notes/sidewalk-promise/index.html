The sidewalk is a promise Los Angeles made in court

A settlement, a $1.4 billion program and three small numbers explain why a sidewalk looks the way it does, and why it is harder to plant than it looks.

By Nate AndersonNov. 20, 20264 min read6 sourcesNote 4 of 5

In short 4 points

  1. A court settlement turned the sidewalk into infrastructure. Los Angeles runs a 30-year, $1.4 billion repair program that began in December 2016.1
  2. Three numbers shape most sidewalks. A clear width of 48 inches, a cross slope of 2.1 percent at most, and vertical steps of a quarter inch or less.6 These are federal guidelines, and I have not checked them against the City's own standards.
  3. They collide with tree planting. A tree needs a lot of soil and the walk needs its 48 clear inches. I found no Los Angeles data on how many defects come from roots.
  4. My rule: draw the clear width, the soil and the slab joint. If I cannot draw all three beside a tree, I say so and do not call it solved.

What I could not verify is listed at the end of the note, before the sources.

01How a settlement became a program

Los Angeles runs a 30-year, $1.4 billion program to make its sidewalks accessible. The city launched it in December 2016.1 The program exists because of a lawsuit, Willits v. City of Los Angeles, in which the plaintiffs alleged the city had failed to properly install and maintain accessible pedestrian facilities.2

The city’s Bureau of Engineering says its Sidewalk Repair Program was initiated in response to the Willits settlement agreement.3 One summary of the proposed agreement put the commitment at approximately $1.37 billion over 30 years.4

The program is still being adjusted. The City Council approved a scoring system to prioritize repairs in January 2018, and in April 2026 approved a new Pedestrian Facility Severity Matrix.1 Part of the program, the Access Request Program, makes repairs requested by or for people with a mobility disability who run into barriers such as broken sidewalks or missing or broken curb ramps.1

The useful thing to take from this is that in Los Angeles a sidewalk is no longer leftover space beside the street. It is a legally tracked piece of infrastructure, and the reason is that people could not use it.

02The three numbers that shape a sidewalk

The federal Access Board’s guidelines for the public right-of-way set the technical rules for what counts as an accessible route. The board issued the final rule in August 2023.5

I will say now that these are guidelines, and I have not checked which version Los Angeles’s own standards currently point to, so I use them here as the clearest published statement of the dimensions, not as the city’s legal requirement. These are the three that matter most for what I draw:

  • Width. The continuous clear width of a pedestrian access route is 48 inches minimum, not counting the curb. Where it is narrower than 60 inches, passing spaces of 60 by 60 inches must be provided at intervals of 200 feet at most.6
  • Slope across the walk. The cross slope of a pedestrian access route outside a crosswalk is 1:48, or 2.1%, at most.6
  • Steps between slabs. Grade breaks must be flush. Changes in level of ¼ inch or less may be vertical. Between ¼ and ½ inch they must be beveled no steeper than 1:2, and above ½ inch up to 6 inches the slope can be 1:12 at most.6

“Changes in level of ¼ inch (6.4 mm) maximum shall be permitted to be vertical.”

U.S. Access Board, Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines, R3 [6]

03Why this matters for shade

Put the two notes together and a tension appears. A tree needs lots of soil (see the previous note), and the sidewalk needs 48 clear inches with no vertical step bigger than a quarter inch. Roots lift slabs. Trunks grow into the clear width. Both are hard to design around when the strip is narrow, and it is why “just plant a tree” is never a complete drawing.

I need to be careful not to overstate that. I know roots are a common reason for lifted slabs, but I have not found Los Angeles data on what share of the city’s sidewalk defects come from tree roots, so I cannot say how big the problem is here. I would not trust anyone who gave me a percentage without a source.

What I take from it as a design rule is concrete. For each tree I propose, my drawing should show the clear width left beside it, the soil it will reach, and what happens at the slab joint if a root lifts it. If I cannot draw those three things, I should say so and not label the tree as solved.

04What I check before I draw a bench or a tree

A sidewalk plan is mostly a list of things that cannot be in the way. Here is the short version of how I now read a site, using the dimensions above.

  • Measure the clear width at the narrowest point, which is usually a pole, a hydrant, a signal box or a tree guard, not the middle of the slab.
  • Look along the joints for steps. A step taller than the quarter inch the guidelines allow to stay vertical is a defect to note, and one beyond half an inch needs a ramped transition.6
  • Check whether the walk is public. On Topanga Canyon Boulevard in my first study, the planting strip is public right-of-way, so anything there is a request to the agencies, not something an owner can just build.
  • Add a bench or tree only where the 48 inches survive. If they do not, the furniture goes on private land behind the walk.

05What I do not know

What I could not verify

  • I read the program’s own pages and the Access Board’s text, not the settlement agreement.
  • I have not found how many miles of sidewalk have been repaired so far, or whether the program is on schedule.
  • I have not checked which accessibility standard the city currently enforces; the numbers above are the federal guideline text.

That list is also my to-do list for a longer note. A real answer to “is the program working?” needs the city’s progress reports, and I have not read them.
