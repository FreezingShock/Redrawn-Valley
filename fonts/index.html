Design system · Type lab

Six ways to draw the type

I tested about 60 hand-drawn, architectural and typewriter fonts for licence, accents, size and how they hold up at caption size, and kept six directions. Each has a full hierarchy on a sample page, and the animation lab below writes text in with a pen or a typewriter, fast and slow. Nothing on the live site has changed.

My read

Pick Hand-cut Serif for the whole site, and borrow Field Notebook for the home page and study titles. Hand fonts are good for titles, labels and notes and poor for long reading: they have small x-heights, uneven rhythm and thin strokes, and they slow the eye after a few hundred words. Hand-cut Serif keeps Newsreader for reading and puts the hand in the title, captions and margin notes. Field Notebook is the bolder choice if you want the whole page to look written. Use the switch below to see any direction with today's Newsreader body.

Direction 4 (Typewriter Log) suits the studies where evidence and numbers lead, and Wobble is the only one where the letters can move on their own.

Direction 1 of 6 · Natural pen handwriting

Field Notebook

Looks like I wrote it on site. Caveat for the title, Kalam for headings, Patrick Hand for the reading text. The most personal of the six and the one closest to a real sketchbook page.

Best for: Home, study titles, short notes, captions.

Watch out for: Caveat has a small x-height and thin strokes, so it needs to be big (the display is 84px at most) and never used for body text. Patrick Hand carries the body, and it is only comfortable up to about 600 words before the eye tires.

Direction 2 of 6 · Architect lettering

Drafting Sheet

Tall, even capitals like a title block on a plan sheet. Amatic SC for the title, Architects Daughter for headings (already on the site as the note font), a round single-story sans for reading.

Best for: Studies and drawings: it makes every page look like a sheet from a set.

Watch out for: Amatic SC is all caps and very narrow, so long headlines get hard to read. Keep titles to about six words. Walter Turncoat is Apache 2.0 (still free for web) and larger than the rest to download.

Direction 3 of 6 · Bold field-sign lettering

Marker and Brush

Fat marker and brush strokes: Caveat Brush for the title, Mansalva for headings, Neucha for reading. Loud and clear from across a room, like a hand-lettered sign taped to a fence.

Best for: Home hero, section openers, anything that needs to feel handmade and confident.

Watch out for: Mansalva is the heaviest here and tires fast in paragraphs, so use it only for headings. Neucha is narrow and lacks × ½ ² ³ (the browser falls back for those).

Direction 4 of 6 · Field log, typed up

Typewriter Log

A survey log typed on a manual machine. Special Elite has real ink spread and uneven keys; Courier Prime keeps the reading text clean. Numbers line up in columns, which suits an evidence-first site.

Best for: Studies, data, captions, sources. It is the natural home for the typewriter animation below.

Watch out for: Monospaced body text runs long: a 68-character line is wider than the current prose measure, so I set it at 17px. Special Elite is Apache 2.0 and 52 KB.

Direction 5 of 6 · Serif with a hand in it

Hand-cut Serif

The calmest option. Fraunces with its soft and wonky axes turned up gives a serif that looks cut by hand; Newsreader stays for reading, so long essays stay comfortable. Hand-drawn character comes from the title, the captions in IM Fell, and Reenie Beanie notes.

Best for: A Wikipedia-style site that still feels drawn. Safest for long reading.

Watch out for: Fraunces with all four axes is 118 KB. The change from today is the smallest, so it may not feel "new" enough.

Direction 6 of 6 · One variable font that moves

Wobble

Shantell Sans is a single variable font drawn like marker handwriting, with axes for how informal it is (INFM) and how much each letter bounces off the baseline (BNCE). One family covers every role, and the letters can be made to wobble live.

Best for: A playful tone and the only direction where the letters themselves can animate, without any stroke data.

Watch out for: At 170 KB with all axes it is the heaviest option (trimming axes would cut it). At body size it reads like a children's workbook; I set INFM low for reading and high for titles.

Animation lab

Writing it in

Three effects, each at two speeds. The pen draws single-stroke letterforms in the order a hand would; the typewriter strikes real fonts key by key; the sweep reveals a real handwriting font as if a pen were passing over it. Press any replay button. With reduced motion turned on in your system, nothing plays by itself and the text is simply shown; a button you press still plays, because you asked for it.

1. Pen write-on

Strokes are drawn one at a time at a steady pen speed (each with a gentle start and stop), with a lift between letters, a small seeded wobble so no two letters match, and a dot for the nib. Ink goes from brick (wet) to dark (dry). Letterforms are the Hershey single-stroke fonts; accents are added by code.

2. Typewriter

Each key lands at its own time (random by about a third either way), pauses a little after commas and longer after full stops, and prints with its own slight baseline drift and ink weight. Characters hold their place before they print, so nothing jumps as lines wrap.

What I could not verify

I did not read each licence file one by one: I used the licence Google Fonts lists for each family and cross-checked it against the fontsource package metadata, which agreed for every font here. Glyph coverage was tested on the default weight, not every weight. I judged legibility by eye on a screen at 375px and 1280px, not with readers, so the “rejected” notes are my opinion. Playwrite’s download size was not measurable from the CSS API.
