Drawing the Valley as a map you can pan

Every study, field guide entry and note that has a place now sits on a hand-drawn map of the San Fernando Valley. This is how the map is drawn, where the pins come from, and what I decided about how it should feel under a thumb or a trackpad.

By Nate AndersonNov. 21, 20266 min read3 sourcesLab 6 of 6

01Why a map

The site is a set of small places in one big valley, but nothing on it showed where any of them are. A reader could finish the Fallbrook study and still not know it is on the opposite side of Woodland Hills from the Topanga one. A map answers that in a glance, and it gives the site a front door that is a picture instead of a list.

It also had to keep itself up to date. I add pages faster than I would remember to move pins, so the map reads the pages instead of a list I maintain by hand.

02How it is drawn

I did not use a map tile service. A tile map would look like every other map and would put a third party in the middle of a site that has no build step and no dependencies. Instead the geography is a short list of coordinates in assets/rds/map-geo.js: the edge of the valley floor, the Los Angeles River and the Tujunga Wash, two flood basins, seven roads and a few dozen place names. A small function turns each list into a wobbly line by nudging points sideways by a seeded random amount and smoothing the result, the same trick the pattern system uses for its banners.

Positions are projected the simple way: longitude is scaled by the cosine of the middle latitude (34.2 degrees) so east-west distances are not stretched. One degree of latitude is about 69 miles,1 so the scale bar is computed from that. The projection is plain equirectangular, the simplest of the families USGS describes.2 Over a 25-mile-wide valley the distortion is far smaller than my error in placing the coastline of the valley by eye.

The hills are rows of tiny ridge marks laid out on a jittered grid and kept out of the valley floor with a point-in-polygon test, which is how hand-drawn maps show mountains. They fade as you zoom in so they do not turn into giant doodles. Everything that has to stay readable, which is pins, names, route shields and the grid labels, is drawn in screen space and repositioned each frame, so text stays the same size at every zoom while the terrain scales beneath it.

03Where the pins come from

A page joins the map by carrying a meta tag in its head: <meta name="rv-geo" content="34.1900,-118.6288">, plus rv-precision (site, street or area) for how sure that spot is. A page about the whole Valley carries rv-scope="valley" instead. Titles, descriptions and topics come from tags the pages already had. scripts/build_index.py reads every page, and scripts/places.py turns the tagged ones into assets/places.json, which the map fetches. Run the build and a new study appears with no change to the map. scripts/check.py warns when a study, guide entry or note has no location tag, so a page cannot quietly miss the map.

The precision tag matters more than it looks. I know the address of the Ventura and Topanga lot, but the Riparian corridor entry is about a whole basin. Giving both the same sharp pin would claim more than I know, so each pin carries a dashed ring for how far off it might be, and the card says so in words.

04The decisions I made

Draw it in code instead of embedding a map service

No tiles, no API key, no third-party requests, and the map matches the sketchbook look. The whole thing is about 70 KB of text.

CostThe geography is approximate. I placed rivers and freeways by eye, so it is good for finding things, not for measuring.

List valley-wide pages instead of faking a pin

Tree canopy and sidewalk law are not anywhere in particular. They sit in a Valley-wide group beside the map and respond to the same filters.

CostTen of nineteen pages are not on the map itself, which makes it look sparser than I would like.

Wheel zoom only after you click the map

A plain scroll over the map scrolls the page, so the map never traps you. Click it, or hold Ctrl or ⌘, and the wheel zooms. Pinch and double-tap zoom on a phone, and there are plus and minus buttons.

CostA first-time visitor has to be told once; a small hint appears the first time the wheel is used over an inactive map.

Make the camera feel physical

Dragging follows the pointer exactly and keeps gliding when you let go. Zoom eases toward the point under the cursor, and selecting a page flies there in 0.7 seconds, zooming in log space so it does not rush at the end. Under reduced motion it jumps instead.

CostIt is a hand-written animation loop, so it is more code to keep than a library call.

Group close pins into a count

Three Woodland Hills pins at full zoom-out would overlap into a smear. Pins within about 34 pixels merge into a numbered badge that zooms in when clicked, and split apart as you approach.

CostA count hides which pages are inside until you hover or zoom.

Keep a list beside the map

The list shows what is in view, highlights its pin on hover, and is the accessible way to reach every page. On a phone it sits under the map and lists everything.

CostIt takes a third of the width on desktop.

05The live map

This is the real map, embedded. Drag to pan, click it and scroll to zoom, hover a pin for a card, click a pin or a list row to fly to it, and use the chips to filter by kind or topic.

06What I could not verify

Limits of this lab

  • The coordinates of rivers, freeways and the valley edge are my estimates from memory of public maps. I could not check them against a survey in this session, so I would not trust the linework closer than about a quarter mile.
  • Pin locations for the studies are placed from their street addresses by hand, not geocoded. Pins marked neighborhood are the middle of an area.
  • I tested pan, zoom, filtering and cards at 375 and 1280 pixels wide in a desktop browser. I did not test on a physical phone or measure frame rate on a slow device.
