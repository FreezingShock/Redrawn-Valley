How every page got its own drawing

Every page and note on this site carries a banner drawn from a seed. This is how I got from a folder of reference sheets to fourteen original motifs, what I decided along the way, and a live lab where you can reroll one.

By NathanNov. 21, 20266 min read4 sourcesLab 1 of 5

01Why generate the art at all

The site started as words and a few diagrams. A study or a note with no picture at the top looks unfinished, but I had three rules in the way: no stock imagery, nothing glossy, and no copying another site’s art. Drawing a banner by hand for every page would also not scale, because every new note would need one.

So the banners are generated from a seed. The page slug is the seed, which means the same page always gets the same drawing, and a different page gets a different one. That is the whole idea. Everything else in this article is about making the generated drawings look like they came from a sketchbook and not from a random-shape toy.

02What I studied

I kept a reference library in the project files, outside the repo, and built contact sheets from it so I could compare many drawings at once. It has two parts. The first is screenshots of public pages from Anthropic’s site, taken on Nov. 20, 2026, for private study.1 The second is public-domain drawings from the Historic American Buildings Survey and its sister programs, made for the National Park Service and held by the Library of Congress. I downloaded them from Wikimedia Commons, where each file is marked public domain, and the library’s own notes record that I checked that through the Commons API on Nov. 20.3

Looking across the sheets, a few habits repeated. The “Building effective agents” hero is a three-tile collage: ink arcs on sage, a drafting compass on green grid paper, a weather-style contour map.1 Anthropic’s designers describe the intent as soft beige, hand-drawn illustration and “the mark of the maker.”2 The survey sheets contribute a different vocabulary: contour rings, tree canopies drawn as circles with a center dot, scale bars, north arrows, double-ruled borders.3

From the contact sheets I wrote down a recipe: two to four flat colors per image plus ink and paper; each tile is one thing; a fine grid sits on a shape and never on the field; drafting tools appear as objects; and ink line weight stays constant inside one drawing. I did not trace anything. The reference pixels stay in the library, and the repo only holds motifs I drew in code.

03The decisions I made

Draw everything in code, from the recipe, not from the pictures

The Anthropic art is copyrighted and the survey sheets are public domain but sheet-specific. Drawing each motif in code from the recipe keeps the site original and keeps the files small (one 35 KB script, not a folder of images). It also means every color is a CSS variable, so the art follows the design tokens.

CostMore work up front, and no motif is as rich as a real engraved sheet.

Seed the randomness with the page slug

The same slug always draws the same banner, so a page does not change its look between visits and a bad composition can be fixed by changing one string. Pages without a topic draw from the full set.

CostI cannot hand-tune a single composition; I can only reroll the seed.

Two palettes, with a firm rule between them

Brick, moss and slate keep their interface jobs: action, link and water. Five extra colors (sky, ochre, rose, kelly and periwinkle), toned from the references, are for decoration only. They never carry text or meaning.

CostThe art accents are not contrast-checked as text colors, so the rule is the safeguard.

Keep the line weight constant and the grid on the shape

This is the most visible habit in the references. It also keeps the generated art from turning into noise when a tile is small.

CostSome compositions look plainer than a freehand drawing would.

Move slowly and rarely

One tile shifts every five to ten seconds: a color crossfade, a disc turning into a rounded square, a needle swinging. It runs only while the art is at least 10% on screen and the tab is visible, and under reduced motion it stays still.

CostThe motion is easy to miss, which is the point, but it means a screenshot never shows it.

Bias the art by topic

A data-topic leans the palette and motifs toward the subject: shade and trees toward moss and canopy plans, water toward slate and contours, heat toward brick and halftone, soil toward ochre.

CostThe mapping is my judgment and is not tested on readers.

04The live lab

This is the same renderer the site uses, with the controls exposed. Type any seed, pick a topic and a layout, press Reroll for a random seed, and watch a tile change its color, shape or icon every few seconds. Pause drift freezes it.

05What I could not verify

Limits of this lab

  • I judged every composition by eye at 375px and 1280px. I did not test the drift on a low-end phone or measure its battery cost; it pauses when off screen and under reduced motion, but that is a design choice, not a measurement.
  • The topic-to-palette mapping is my taste. No reader has been asked whether a heat banner “feels” like heat.
  • I describe the reference art from what I saw on the pages. I could not read the artists’ intent beyond what the Kinfolk piece says.
